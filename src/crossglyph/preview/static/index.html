How much ink a glyph gets. FreeType draws each pixel with a coverage from nothing to full, and this bends that scale before the four shades are picked off it. Over 1 darkens, under 1 lightens, and 1 leaves it alone.

The usual first move on an e-ink screen, where a face tuned for paper or a backlit display often sets too light. Up to about 2 it darkens. Past 3 it darkens barely at all and mostly turns grey edges solid black.

It bends how much white is left rather than how much ink there is, so a bigger number is a darker page. Plain gamma arithmetic runs the other way, and so would surprise anyone who has met this setting in a reader before.

The 0.3 to 4 range is CoolReader's, which offers 48 fixed steps over the same curve, computed the same way. The steps there crowd around 1, where the eye can tell 1.02 from 1.05, and spread out at the top, where 3.8 and 4 look alike. Here it is a plain number instead, and the arrows step it by 0.05, which is that scale's finest step.
Which of the device's four shades each pixel ends up: paper, light grey, dark grey or black. FreeType draws a glyph in sixteen shades of coverage, and these three cut points say where the boundaries fall. Under the first is paper. At or over the third is solid black.

Lower cut points put more ink on the page. darkened is what the device's own fonts use, and darkest is a step past it.

The first number does the most work. With the reader's anti-aliasing off it is the only one that does any: the device paints every shade above it solid black, so the other two have nothing left to decide.

If the page is simply too light, try gamma first. That changes how much ink there is, where these only place what is already drawn. A config can set its own three, and the row then carries them as a fourth entry.
How the outline is fitted to the pixel grid. A letter's outline rarely lands on whole pixels, so an upright stem can straddle two of them and come out as two pale columns instead of one dark one. Hinting nudges the outline until it lands cleanly.

normal runs the font's own hinting instructions where it carries them, and lets FreeType work it out where it does not. light fits vertically only, which keeps the shapes the designer drew and lets the widths fall where they may. none fits nothing. auto has FreeType fit the font whatever instructions it carries, and is worth trying on a face that looks muddy at small sizes.

This row also decides what two of the switches below it can do. grayscale hinting is a choice between two ways of running the font's own instructions, so it needs a mode where those run, which for almost every font means normal. stem darkening reaches a TrueType face only under light. Each greys itself out when the pair you have chosen leaves it nothing to do.
Which of FreeType's two engines runs a font's own hinting. A font can carry hinting instructions, a small program that nudges each outline so its stems land on whole pixels instead of straddling two of them.

The engine used by default was written for colour LCD screens, where fitting a stem sideways costs more than it gains, so it fits vertically only. This one fits both directions, which is what a grey screen wants: a stem lands on one pixel rather than being spread over two in grey.

It reaches a face only while that face's own instructions are what draw it. A font carrying none, and any font under light, auto or none hinting, is fitted by FreeType's own auto-hinter instead. The row greys itself in those cases and says which one it is. •
Builds the font with no greys in it at all. Every pixel comes out paper or ink, chosen by FreeType's dropout control, a rule that keeps a stroke too thin to land on a pixel from disappearing altogether. Nothing measures coverage and cuts it at the thresholds.

Worth trying for a reader with anti-aliasing off. The device paints every shade above the first cut point solid black, so a pixel a quarter covered goes black and strokes fatten into each other. Picking the pixels for a two level page directly leaves the letters open.

It belongs to the font and not to the page: one built this way draws in two levels whatever the reader's anti-aliasing is set to. gamma and thresholds have no coverage left to work on while it is on, so both grey out. Fitting to whole pixels also moves some advance widths, so lines break in slightly different places. •
Thickens the strokes a little, to make up for how thin type looks once it is drawn small. Spacing does not move, so the page darkens without setting to different lines.

Where it applies depends on the hinting row as much as on the font. FreeType darkens in two places, and each adds a condition of its own: the engine that draws CFF and OpenType outlines darkens a glyph loaded at a size, and the auto-hinter darkens at a light target. So a CFF or OTF face moves under any hinting but auto, a TrueType face moves only under light, and nothing moves under auto. The row greys itself on those two cases.

The amount differs as much as the reach does. Through the CFF engine it is slight, well under a percent of the inked pixels. Through the light auto-hinter it is substantial. A face left live can still come out unchanged, its stems falling where the curve rounds to nothing, and weight is the control that will move it. •
Whether a pair such as fi is drawn as one shape. A font can carry rules for that, and the build reads them and writes the joined shapes into the file.

Turn them off when a face's fi or fl comes out as a blot at reading sizes. Four shades of grey are far less forgiving than paper, and the hood of the f meeting the dot of the i is exactly the detail that goes muddy.

The shapes are baked into each style at build time, so this is not something the reader can change later. A family carrying no ligature rules greys the row. •
Which of the two digit widths the font is built with. Tabular pads every digit out to a common width so that columns of numbers line up, and it is what most fonts hand out by default. Proportional lets each digit take its own width, so a 1 is narrower than a 0.

A book is prose and not a table, and there the padding reads as a gap around every narrow digit. The sample text carries 111 118 181 811 1118 so the difference is visible rather than taken on trust.

It applies the font's own proportional figures while the glyphs are drawn, so the device never knows about it. That means it moves only a font whose designer drew them, and only the digits they drew: some faces substitute all ten, some a handful, most none at all. A family with none greys the row.

Base coverage is in every build first: ASCII and basic Latin plus Unicode punctuation, which keeps letters, numbers, spaces, smart quotes, dashes and ellipses available without adding a language range. What you tick is added on top of it.

Reading: Default plus the ranges English fiction and popular science actually reach for: broader Latin, Greek terms like λ and φ, box and geometric symbols, supplemental arrows, uncommon dialogue punctuation, CJK quote marks, music notes (♪ ♫ ♬), stars (★) and dingbats (✓).
